Book of the week: The Goal, Goldratt and Cox

This week's book is The Goal by Eliyahu M. Goldratt and Jeff Cox, the 1984 business novel that introduced the Theory of Constraints. Its main idea: find the bottleneck, protect it and pace everything else to it, because an hour lost there is lost for the whole system. For planners, plant managers and network designers.

The big ideas

1:49Narrator: The first big idea is the question itself. Jonah's answer is that the goal of a manufacturing company is to make money, now and in the future.

2:00Narrator: Efficiency, quality and new machines are means to that end, and never the goal on their own.

2:07Narrator: To keep score, Jonah throws out cost per part and uses three measures. In Goldratt's words, throughput is the rate at which the system generates money through sales.

2:19Narrator: Inventory is all the money tied up in things the company intends to sell. Operating expense is the money spent turning inventory into throughput.

2:29Narrator: The order of the three matters. Most cost programmes start with operating expense, because it's easy to see. Goldratt starts with throughput, because cost has a floor and sales have no ceiling.

2:42Narrator: In planning terms, a pallet of finished goods with no order behind it isn't output. It's cash sitting in a warehouse, waiting for a forecast to come true.

2:53Narrator: The second idea comes from the book's best known scene, a scout hike. Alex leads his son's troop, and the line keeps stretching out.

3:03Narrator: Each boy's pace varies, and nobody can walk faster than the boy in front of him. The whole troop moves at the speed of the slowest boy, Herbie, and the gaps in the line keep growing.

3:15Narrator: Goldratt's point is that two things together do the damage, steps that depend on each other and normal variation in each step. Delays don't average out down the line, they pile up.

3:28Narrator: So a perfectly balanced plant, where every machine has just enough capacity for demand, is a trap. Alex fixes the hike by putting Herbie at the front and sharing out the load in his pack.

3:42Narrator: In planning terms, that's the case for spare capacity outside the constraint. A network that runs every site and lane close to full will lose output to normal variation, even when the averages add up.

3:56Narrator: The third idea is bottleneck arithmetic. Back at the plant, Alex's team finds two Herbies, the N C X ten machine and heat treatment.

4:06Narrator: On the books, an hour of that machine costs about thirty dollars. Jonah works out that an hour lost there really costs the plant around two thousand seven hundred dollars, because the whole plant's output waits on it.

4:21Narrator: So the team stops feeding the bottleneck parts that would fail inspection later, makes sure it never stands idle, and brings old machines back into use to take some of its load.

4:33Narrator: Everywhere else the logic runs the other way. An hour saved at a machine that isn't a bottleneck adds nothing, and running it flat out only builds a pile of work in front of Herbie.

4:45Narrator: Smaller batches follow from the same logic. Alex's team cuts batch sizes in half, which means more setups on machines with time to spare and far less waiting in queues.

4:57Narrator: The catch, and Goldratt makes you feel it, is that the old efficiency reports look worse while the plant gets better. Alex has to defend that to bosses who still read those reports.

5:09Narrator: The fourth idea turns all this into a method. Alex's team releases material to the floor only as fast as the bottleneck can work through it.

5:19Narrator: Goldratt later named this drum, buffer, rope, in The Race, a book he wrote with Robert Fox. The bottleneck's schedule is the drum, a small stock of work in front of it is the buffer, and the release rule is the rope.

5:35Narrator: Near the end of the novel, the lessons become five focusing steps. Identify the constraint, exploit it, subordinate everything else to it, elevate it, and then start again.

5:48Narrator: The last step comes with a warning, don't let inertia become the constraint. Once the bottleneck moves, yesterday's rules are the next thing holding you back.

5:59Narrator: In the story the constraint does move, out of the plant and into the market. Alex goes after a European order at lower prices, so spare capacity earns money without cutting prices at home.

6:13Narrator: For planners, step three is the hard one. Subordinating means most of the organisation stops chasing its own numbers, and that changes how people are measured as well as how work is scheduled.

Why it matters now

6:27Narrator: Does the book still hold up forty years on? The core logic does. Every supply chain has a constraint, and planning around it beats treating every resource as equally important.

6:39Narrator: Sales and operations planning, S and OP, asks the question Jonah asks. Where is the constraint this month, and what do we do with the capacity that isn't?

6:50Narrator: The book also explains why high utilisation targets so often make lead times worse. Plenty of plants and warehouses still haven't learned that lesson.

7:01Narrator: Where it has dated is the setting. It's one plant, in the early nineteen eighties, with two clear bottlenecks that stay put.

7:09Narrator: Today's constraints sit just as often with a supplier, a port, a planner's time or a policy. In a network they move with the season and the product mix.

7:20Narrator: Fair critics make this point too. Christoph Roser, who writes the All About Lean blog, argues that bottlenecks usually shift in practice, and that drum, buffer, rope only protects the bottleneck from running out of work.

7:35Narrator: He prefers kanban, or a simple cap on total work in progress. He also argues that Goldratt overstated his methods and gave too little credit to earlier ideas.

7:48Narrator: The novel form has a cost as well. The marriage story is thin, and the lessons arrive neatly one by one, so the method looks simpler on the page than it is on a real shop floor.
